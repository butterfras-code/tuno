Yeah — tuNotes fits really well, and I think the strongest version is less “music theory worksheet inside tUno” and more a tiny arcade game for note fluency.

The core loop could be very simple: a note appears, the student identifies it, Uno reacts, and the game quietly adjusts around them. That keeps it feeling like the rest of tUno instead of becoming a settings-heavy theory app. The dog-treat idea is especially good because streaks can become part of the character language: small streak = tail wag, longer streak = happy animation, bigger milestone = treat toss/toast, maybe a truly long streak triggers a rare “catch” animation.

I’d separate the experience into three broad ways to play, without exposing a million configuration knobs:

Practice — choose a sensible preset such as “Beginner Treble,” “Clarinet Range,” “Low Brass,” “Bass Clef Basics,” etc.
Adaptive — starts from a preset, then notices which notes are slow or frequently missed and feeds those back in more often.
Versus — two students share one screen, probably mirrored or side-by-side, each with their own note stream and score/streak.
For the presets, I’d avoid making students configure staff range, clef, accidentals, ledger lines, answer method, etc. individually unless they open an advanced setup. A band teacher should be able to say “Open tuNotes → Beginning Band → Treble Set 2” and everyone is playing in seconds.

I’d think of Challenge as a shell around the same note-reading engine, with different round formats inside it.

I’d collapse it to two actual game types, and treat everything else as player/layout configuration:

Timed — play for X seconds. Scoring can be either Correct Notes or Correct Notes × Accuracy.
Target — reach X correct answers. Winner is lowest completion time, with an accuracy floor so button-spamming doesn’t work.
Then head-to-head, heats, pass-and-play, and relay are not separate modes at all. They’re just different ways of scheduling the selected players through one of those two game types. That’s cleaner for both the UI and the code.

For the timed scoring, I like Notes × Accuracy because it naturally punishes guessing without needing a pile of penalties. If someone gets 30 correct at 80%, they score 24; 25 correct at 100% scores 25. Easy to explain to kids, too.

For Target mode, I’d probably avoid a harsh “you failed because accuracy dipped below 85%” rule. Better might be: require something like 80% accuracy to post an official time. If they finish under that, they get a result such as “Finished — accuracy too low to rank,” and can retry. That keeps the anti-spam safeguard without making the game suddenly stop mid-round.

The relay variant gets interesting because the team could share one cumulative target or cumulative timed score, while individual stats still exist underneath.
